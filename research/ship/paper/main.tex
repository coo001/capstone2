\begin{abstract}
SHIP (Cheon, Hanrot, Kim and Stehl\'e, EUROCRYPT 2025) is a CKKS bootstrapping algorithm that replaces the top-level homomorphic DFT and the polynomial approximation of modular reduction by $h$ independent blind rotations and a product tree, using only $6$ levels for a sparse secret of Hamming weight $h=31$.
We report, to the best of our knowledge, the first publicly available implementation of SHIP, as a feature of a fork of OpenFHE~1.4.2 with a \texttt{CryptoContext} API, key serialization, seed-compressed keys ($0.50\times$) and disk-backed keys.
We run the four parameter sets of the paper with small, documented deviations: LL14 and HT15 are within $0.7$ bits of the published median precision, HT14 is $0.5$ bits below, and LL13 reaches $3.1$ bits ($4.3$ with secret-key encryption; paper: $4.45$) after moving one bit per product-tree prime to the auxiliary prime within the same budget.
Three findings go beyond reproduction.
(1)~At SHIP's small scales, the approximate ModDown of hybrid key switching floors instead of rounding; exact centered rounding improves LL14 by $0.5$ bits.
(2)~For the sparse encapsulation key, the lattice-estimator version cited by the SHIP authors rates $(\log N,\log(Pq_0))=(13,55)$ at $2^{143.6}$ under its default attacks but at $2^{116.8}$ under the dual meet-in-the-middle hybrid, and $(14,100)$ at $2^{119.1}$. Shrinking the special prime fixes this but costs precision (LL14 drops to $8$ bits). We therefore decompose the encapsulation key into $8$-bit digits, which lets the special prime shrink to $17$ bits without precision loss: the new defaults reach $2^{135.6}$--$2^{155.7}$ under every attack for LL14, HT14 and HT15 and $2^{127.6}$ for LL13. An entropy-matched model of SHIP's windowed secret lowers these estimates by $8$--$15$ bits and leaves LL13 and LL14 below $2^{128}$, which identifies the windowed secret as the remaining weak point; widening LL14's window from $264$ to $396$ restores $2^{128}$ under that model at $28\%$ more latency.
(3)~Against OpenFHE's bootstrapping tuned with the same sparse-secret encapsulation and an equal number of slots, SHIP LL14 is $1.6$--$2.0\times$ faster at $4$--$15$ threads on a $15$-core laptop CPU, but slower on one thread, $4$ bits less precise and not more memory-efficient.
SHIP's practical advantage is multi-threaded latency at a four times smaller ring degree.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%=====================================================================

CKKS~\cite{CKKS17} offers high-throughput approximate arithmetic on encrypted vectors, but its bootstrapping~\cite{CHKKS18} is expensive in latency.
The conventional procedure evaluates a homomorphic DFT at the top modulus, a polynomial approximation of the modular reduction, and an inverse DFT.
In OpenFHE these steps consume $15$ to $21$ levels in the configurations we measured, which forces ring degree $N=2^{16}$ at 128-bit security for full-slot bootstrapping with good precision.

SHIP~\cite{CHKS25} changes this balance.
Writing the decryption equation $a s + b = m \bmod q_0$ coefficient-wise, it computes $\omega^{m_i}$ with $\omega=e^{2\pi\iota/q_0}$ ($\iota=\sqrt{-1}$) as a product of factors, one per nonzero coefficient of a sparse secret of Hamming weight $h$.
Each factor is obtained by a \emph{blind rotation} of public phases by an encrypted index, and the $h+1$ terms are multiplied in a binary tree.
Only the tree consumes levels, $\lceil\log_2(h+1)\rceil=5$ for $h=31$, plus one level for the slots-to-coefficients transform (S2C).
With $6$ levels, the authors report the first CKKS-only full-slot bootstrapping at $N=2^{13}$, $215$\,ms on $32$ cores, and good multi-core scaling because the $h$ factors are independent.

The original evaluation used an experimental implementation on the HEaaN library~\cite{HEaaN}.
We are not aware of a public implementation, and several practical questions are open: how SHIP behaves inside a general-purpose library whose key-switching and scaling conventions were designed for $40$--$60$-bit primes; whether its sparse-key parameters hold under the attacks implemented in the lattice estimator; how large its keys are; and how it compares with a \emph{tuned} conventional bootstrapping on the same machine.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Implementation (\S\ref{sec:impl}).} SHIP as an OpenFHE feature: column-plus-mux blind rotations with base-$4$ multiplexers and hoisting, the windowed sparse secret, masks multiplied at the auxiliary modulus (no masking level), sparse-secret encapsulation at modulus $q_0p'$, a one-level dense S2C, and role-specific primes. We add key serialization, seed compression and disk-backed keys, which make the $70$\,GiB HT15 key usable on a $48$\,GB machine.
\item \textbf{Rounding in ModDown (\S\ref{sec:moddown}).} OpenFHE's approximate ModDown floors instead of rounding. We add exact centered rounding behind OpenFHE's \texttt{WITH\_REDUCED\_NOISE} flag; all $2{,}119$ OpenFHE unit tests pass.
\item \textbf{Security re-evaluation and a fix (\S\ref{sec:security}, \S\ref{sec:digits}).} We evaluate the sparse-key bounds of the paper with the estimator version cited by its authors and with the current one, including the dual meet-in-the-middle hybrid that is not part of the default attack set. Two of the three published bounds fall to $2^{117}$--$2^{120}$ under that attack. A digit-decomposed encapsulation key removes the precision cost of a small special prime, so the bounds can be tightened by $13$--$55$ bits without precision loss. We also estimate the effect of the windowed secret structure.
\item \textbf{Evaluation (\S\ref{sec:eval}).} We run LL13, LL14, HT14 and HT15, and compare against OpenFHE's \texttt{EvalBootstrap} with sparse-secret encapsulation~\cite{BTH22}, tuned over level budgets and slot counts, at $1$, $4$ and $15$ threads, with per-phase profiles, measured key sizes and the cost of noise flooding.
\end{itemize}
The code, unit tests, raw logs and estimator scripts are available\coderef.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
%=====================================================================

\paragraph{CKKS and RNS}
We use the RNS variant of CKKS with ciphertext modulus $Q=\prod_{i=0}^{L} q_i$, auxiliary modulus $P$ for hybrid key switching with $d_{\mathrm{num}}$ digits, ring $\R=\Z[X]/(X^N+1)$ and $N/2$ complex slots.
With OpenFHE's \texttt{FIXEDMANUAL} scaling, every rescaling prime is assumed equal to the scaling factor $\Delta=2^p$.
Security of the dense (uniform ternary) secret follows the HE standard~\cite{HEStd18}, which bounds $\log_2(QP)$ for each $N$ ($218$, $438$, $881$ and $1747$ bits for $N=2^{13},\dots,2^{16}$).

\paragraph{Sparse-secret encapsulation}
Bootstrapping prefers a sparse secret at the bottom modulus, but sparse secrets weaken the RLWE instance.
Bossuat et al.~\cite{BTH22} keep the dense secret everywhere and switch to a sparse secret only around ModRaise, with one switching key at a small modulus; only that modulus must satisfy the sparse-secret security bound.
OpenFHE exposes this as \texttt{SPARSE\_ENCAPSULATED} ($h=32$, key modulus $q_0p_0$ with $p_0$ the first auxiliary prime).
SHIP uses the same idea with $h=31$ and a dedicated special prime $p'$; the paper writes the modulus as $P\cdot q_0$, and we write $q_0p'$.

\paragraph{SHIP}
Let $(a,b)\in\R_{q_0}^2$ decrypt to $m$ under a sparse secret $s$ with nonzero coefficients $s_{j_1},\dots,s_{j_h}\in\{\pm1\}$; the coefficients encode messages $\mu_i\approx\gamma m_i/q_0$ with gap $\gamma=q_0/\Delta$.
For every coefficient $i$,
\[
\omega^{m_i} = \omega^{b_i}\prod_{k=1}^{h}\omega^{\pm a_{i-j_k} s_{j_k}},
\]
with signs from the negacyclic reduction.
SHIP evaluates this product for all slots at once~\cite[Alg.~1]{CHKS25}:
(i)~the public phases $\omega^{\pm a}$ are encoded in four half-vectors;
(ii)~for each nonzero $s_{j_k}$, encrypted masks that depend on $(j_k,s_{j_k})$ select and sign the right halves~\cite[Lemma~1]{CHKS25};
(iii)~a blind rotation by the encrypted index $j_k$ aligns the factor;
(iv)~a product tree multiplies the $h$ factors and the public term $\frac{\gamma}{4\pi\iota}\omega^{b}$;
(v)~adding the complex conjugate yields $\frac{\gamma}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi\mu_i/\gamma)\approx\mu_i$ (scaled by $\Delta$) in the slots.
The blind rotation combines a \emph{column} method, where the masks are pre-rotated for $\theta$ candidate offsets, and a \emph{mux} method, where encrypted digits in base $B$ drive $B$-to-$1$ multiplexed rotations~\cite[Def.~1, Alg.~3--5]{CHKS25}.
To bound the rotation range, the secret has one nonzero coefficient per window: the $k$-th lies in $[kN/h-w,\,kN/h+w)$, so only $2w$ positions must be covered.
The paper argues the security of this structure with May's meet-LWE estimate~\cite{May21}, requiring $(4w)^{0.4h}\ge 2^{115}$~\cite[\S5.1]{CHKS25}.
Masks are multiplied at modulus $QP$ with scale $P$ and removed by the ModDown of key switching, so masking consumes no level~\cite[\S4.1, \S4.4]{CHKS25}.
A dense S2C at the bottom modulus moves the slots into coefficients with one level~\cite[\S3.2]{CHKS25}.
The paper defines four parameter sets~\cite[Table~2]{CHKS25}: LL13 and LL14 (low latency, one level left after bootstrapping) and HT14 and HT15 (high throughput, nine levels).

\section{Implementation in OpenFHE}
\label{sec:impl}
%=====================================================================

The implementation is a new module \texttt{ckksrns-ship} (about $1{,}400$ lines of C++) in a fork of OpenFHE~1.4.2, plus five methods on \texttt{CryptoContext}; it is not part of upstream OpenFHE.
\begin{verbatim}
auto spec = SHIPContextSpec::LL14();
auto cc   = GenSHIPCryptoContext(spec);
auto kp   = cc->KeyGen();
cc->EvalMultKeyGen(kp.secretKey);
auto prm  = SHIPParams::Recommended(spec);
cc->EvalSHIPBootstrapKeyGen(kp.secretKey, prm);
auto out  = cc->EvalSHIPBootstrap(ct);
\end{verbatim}
\texttt{GenSHIPCryptoContext} builds the modulus chain and throws if $\log_2(QP)$ exceeds the HE-standard bound.
The input is a fully packed, rescaled ciphertext with at least two limbs; the output has the full chain minus the five product-tree levels.

\subsection{Data flow}
\texttt{EvalSHIPBootstrap} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:ship}) drops the input to two limbs, applies the dense S2C with a baby-step giant-step diagonal method (one level), switches to the sparse secret at modulus $q_0p'$, and runs the half bootstrap for real messages (twice for complex messages).
The $h=31$ factors are computed in an OpenMP parallel loop.
For each factor, the four public phase polynomials are lifted from $q_0$ to all limbs of $QP$, rotated to the $\theta$ column offsets, multiplied by the column keys $\mathrm{Enc}_{QP}(P\cdot\text{mask})$, summed, and reduced by one ModDown.
The mux part has $\lceil\log_B\lceil 2w/\theta\rceil\rceil$ steps (three in all presets), each a $B$-to-$1$ mux-rotate whose $B$ branches share one gadget decomposition and one ModDown.
The product tree multiplies the $32$ terms; the public term enters as a trivial ciphertext, so evaluation needs no public key.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\texttt{EvalSHIPBootstrap} for real messages}
\label{alg:ship}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require fully packed ciphertext $ct$ with $\ge 2$ limbs; factor keys $C_k$, $M_k$ ($1\le k\le h$); input bound $K$; tree scale correction $\kappa$ (\S\ref{sec:primes})
\State $ct \gets$ \textsc{LevelReduce}$(ct)$ to two limbs
\State $ct \gets$ \textsc{S2C}$(ct)$ \Comment{one level; now modulo $q_0$}
\State $(a,b) \gets$ \textsc{Encapsulate}$(ct)$ \Comment{dense $\to$ sparse at $q_0p'$}
\State $\phi_{1..4} \gets$ halves of $\omega^{\pm a}$, encoded at $q_0$ and lifted to $QP$
\State $T_0 \gets$ trivial ciphertext of $\frac{\gamma K}{4\pi\iota\kappa}\,\omega^{b}$
\ForAll{$k=1,\dots,h$ \textbf{in parallel}}
  \State $c \gets \sum_{t<\theta}\sum_{u=1}^{4} \textsc{Rot}_{o_k+t}(\phi_u)\cdot C_{k,t,u}$ \Comment{at $QP$}
  \State $c \gets \textsc{ModDown}_P(c)$ \Comment{masking uses no level}
  \For{each of the $\lceil\log_B\lceil 2w/\theta\rceil\rceil$ mux steps $\ell$}
    \State $c \gets \textsc{MuxRot}_B(c, M_{k,\ell})$ \Comment{shared decomposition}
  \EndFor
  \State $T_k \gets c$
\EndFor
\State $R \gets \textsc{ProductTree}(T_0,\dots,T_h)$ \Comment{$\lceil\log_2(h+1)\rceil$ levels}
\State \Return $R + \textsc{Conj}(R)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Role-specific primes and exact output scale}
\label{sec:primes}
The paper assigns different prime sizes to the base prime, the S2C prime, the user's primes, the product-tree primes and the auxiliary primes~\cite[Table~2]{CHKS25}.
We choose for each role the NTT-friendly prime closest to the target size.
Two corrections make this work under \texttt{FIXEDMANUAL}:
\begin{itemize}
\item The product tree is computed at scale $2^{b}$ with $b>p$, while downstream code expects $2^{p}$. We set the last tree prime to $2b-p$ bits so that the root returns to scale $2^p$.
\item Small NTT-friendly primes deviate from $2^b$ by a few percent, and the deviation compounds through five tree levels. All leaves enter at the same level with known scale, so the true root scale $\kappa\cdot 2^p$ is deterministic; we simulate the pairing once and fold $1/\kappa$ into the public term. Likewise, the S2C diagonals are multiplied by $q/\Delta$ for the exact prime $q$ they are rescaled by.
\end{itemize}
OpenFHE generates $60$-bit auxiliary primes on 64-bit builds (compile-time constant \texttt{AUXMODSIZE}); to fit LL13's $218$-bit budget, the SHIP context regenerates the CRT tables with the requested auxiliary prime size.
Table~\ref{tab:keys} lists the resulting parameters and their deviations from the paper.

\subsection{Keys}
\label{sec:keys}
A SHIP key holds, per nonzero coefficient, $4\theta$ column ciphertexts and $3B\,d_{\mathrm{num}}$ mux key components at modulus $QP$, plus S2C rotation keys, the conjugation key and the encapsulation key.
Three measures keep it manageable:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Level truncation.} S2C and conjugation keys are used at one known level, so their digits and limbs are restricted to that level.
\item \textbf{Seed compression.} Every uniform component $a$ is derived from a per-key random seed (BLAKE2) and an identifier; the compact serialization stores only the $b$ components. The measured LL14 key is $10.18$\,GiB full and $5.09$\,GiB compact. The seed determines only public components; both sides must use the same PRNG.
\item \textbf{Disk-backed factor keys.} With a directory argument, the factor keys are written to one file per factor and streamed during bootstrapping; only the S2C, conjugation and encapsulation keys stay in memory.
\end{itemize}
For comparison, OpenFHE's bootstrapping keys at $N=2^{16}$ (rotation, conjugation and encapsulation keys, level budget $(2,2)$) serialize to $11.1$\,GiB for full packing and $9.1$\,GiB for $8{,}192$ slots, without seed compression.

\subsection{Digit-decomposed encapsulation key}
\label{sec:digits}
The dense-to-sparse switch multiplies the $q_0$ component $a$ of the ciphertext by a key $\mathrm{Enc}_{s'}(p'\cdot s)$ modulo $q_0p'$ and divides by $p'$, so its noise is about $\|a\|\cdot\|e\|\sqrt{N}/p'\approx (q_0/p')\,\sigma\sqrt{N}$.
With $p'\ge q_0$ this is negligible, which is why the paper's bounds put $\lgqp$ at roughly twice $\log_2 q_0$; with $p'<q_0$ it grows by one bit per bit removed from $p'$ (LL14: $16.2$ bits at $\lgqp=88$, $12.1$ at $80$, $8.1$ at $76$; Table~\ref{tab:sparse}).
We apply the standard gadget decomposition to this one key: $a$ is split into $k=\lceil\log_2 q_0/d\rceil$ signed digits of $d$ bits, and the key holds $\mathrm{Enc}_{s'}(p'\,2^{jd}s)$ for $j<k$.
The noise becomes about $k\,2^{d-1}\sigma\sqrt{N}/p'$, independent of $q_0$, at the cost of $k$ instead of one ring products at the bottom modulus and $k$ RLWE samples under the sparse secret; the estimator's default of unlimited samples covers this, and restricting it to the $7n$ samples of LL13's decomposed key gives the same $2^{127.6}$.
With $d=8$ and the smallest NTT-friendly $p'$ ($17$ bits), LL14 keeps $16.2$--$16.5$ bits at $\lgqp=65$ ($16.0$--$16.4$ bits with $d=12$ or $16$ at $76$), against $8.1$ bits without decomposition.
The presets now use $d=8$ and $p'$ of $17$ bits, i.e.\ $\lgqp=42$, $65$, $45$ and $68$ for LL13, LL14, HT14 and HT15.
The additional key material is $k$ polynomial pairs modulo $q_0p'$ (at most $7$), i.e.\ a few hundred kilobytes.

\subsection{Input range and testing}
SHIP computes $\frac{\gamma}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi\mu/\gamma)$, which is close to $\mu$ only for $|\mu|\ll\gamma$.
A parameter \texttt{messageBound}~$K$ divides the message by $K$ inside the S2C diagonals and multiplies it back through the public term, at no level cost and about $\log_2 K$ bits of precision.
On inputs in $[-64,64]$, $K=1$ gives a maximum error of $1.62$ and $K=64$ gives $4.0\times10^{-4}$.
Nine unit tests cover real and complex messages, windowed and unrestricted secrets, base-$2$ and base-$4$ mux, role-specific primes, inputs at higher levels, invalid inputs and keys, compact serialization round trips, disk-backed keys, the input range, and the HE-standard check of the presets.
A client--server example generates and serializes the key on one side and bootstraps, squares and bootstraps again on the other.

\section{Exact Rounding in ModDown}
\label{sec:moddown}
%=====================================================================

Hybrid key switching ends with $\mathrm{ModDown}$: given $x$ over $QP$, compute $\lfloor x/P\rceil$ over $Q$.
OpenFHE's \texttt{ApproxModDown} obtains $[x]_P$ by a fast basis conversion, $\tilde r=\sum_j y_j\hat P_j$ with $\hat P_j=P/p_j$ and $y_j=[x\,\hat P_j^{-1}]_{p_j}$, and returns $(x-\tilde r)/P$.
Two effects make this a biased estimate of $x/P$:
(1)~$[x]_P\in[0,P)$, so the division is a floor, with mean error $1/2$; and
(2)~$\tilde r = [x]_P + \alpha P$ with $\alpha\in[0,k)$ for $k$ auxiliary primes, an extra integer error.
In key switching the error is multiplied by the secret.
A constant error $c$ per coefficient multiplied by a dense ternary secret gives coefficients that are signed partial sums of $c\,s$, of standard deviation about $c\sqrt{2N/3}$, i.e.\ about $37$ for $c=1/2$ and $N=2^{13}$.
At scales of $2^{40}$ or more this is negligible.
SHIP, however, runs hundreds of key switches at scales of $2^{20}$--$2^{24}$ (LL13); in our diagnostics every factor carried an error of $0.02$--$0.25$ in slot~$0$.

With \texttt{WITH\_REDUCED\_NOISE} and plaintext modulus $t=0$ (CKKS, BFV), we compute the centered remainder exactly,
\[
r=\sum_j y_j \hat P_j - \Big\lfloor \sum_j \tfrac{y_j}{p_j} \Big\rceil P,
\]
with a $128$-bit accumulator and Barrett reduction, so that $(x-r)/P=\lfloor x/P\rceil$.
BGV ($t>0$) and builds without the flag are unchanged, and all OpenFHE unit tests (core $156$, binfhe $78$, pke $1{,}885$ including the SHIP tests) pass with the flag enabled.

\section{Security Re-evaluation}
\label{sec:security}
%=====================================================================

SHIP involves three secrets: the dense ternary secret of all evaluation keys at modulus $QP$, the sparse secret of the encapsulation key at modulus $q_0p'$, and the windowed position structure of that sparse secret.
We used the lattice estimator~\cite{APS15,estimator} at commit \texttt{14a3625} (2024-09-30), which is the version cited by the SHIP authors, and at commit \texttt{d8c00b48} (2026-08-19), both with SageMath~10.5, the default MATZOV reduction cost model~\cite{MATZOV22}, error $\sigma=3.19$ and an unlimited number of samples (restricting to $m=n$ did not change the result we tested).
The sparse secret is modelled as $16$ coefficients $+1$ and $15$ coefficients $-1$.
Costs are $\log_2$ of \texttt{rop}, which ignores memory; the meet-in-the-middle attacks need about $2^{77}$ memory at $(13,55)$.

\paragraph{Criterion}
For dense keys we follow the HE standard, as the paper does.
The current estimator rates the HE-standard boundary itself at $2^{125.9}$--$2^{126.1}$, and our dense keys are at or above it (Table~\ref{tab:dense}).
For the sparse key we require $2^{128}$ under every attack and both estimator versions; a point between $2^{126}$ and $2^{128}$ is therefore as secure as the standard dense parameters by the same tool, but does not meet our stricter criterion.

\paragraph{Sparse key}
The paper states that $(\log N,\log(Pq_0))=(13,55)$, $(14,100)$ and $(15,105)$ give 128-bit security for $h=31$~\cite[\S5.2]{CHKS25}.
Table~\ref{tab:sparse} reports the two hybrid attacks that dominate at these small moduli; uSVP does not apply and the plain dual hybrid is more expensive.
With the cited version, the default attacks of \texttt{LWE.estimate} (which include the primal hybrid but not the dual meet-in-the-middle hybrid) give $2^{143.6}$ at $(13,55)$, consistent with the paper's claim, but the dual meet-in-the-middle hybrid gives $2^{116.8}$.
At $(14,100)$ the same attack gives $2^{119.1}$.
With the current version, the dual attack gives $2^{117.7}$ and $2^{119.9}$, and the primal hybrid itself drops to $2^{121.4}$ and $2^{126.2}$.
The old version's primal hybrid raised an internal error at several of the smaller moduli, which the current version no longer does.
Hybrid attacks on sparse secrets have been revised repeatedly~\cite{SC19,CP19,SecGuide24}, and recent GPU implementations of the primal hybrid support the estimator's predictions in practice~\cite{PV25}.

\paragraph{Windowed positions}
The estimator models a generic sparse ternary secret, whereas SHIP's secret has one nonzero coefficient per window.
This removes entropy: $\log_2\big(\binom{N}{h}2^h\big)$ versus $h\log_2(4w)$, i.e.\ $321.3$ versus $293.0$ bits for LL13 and $352.3$ versus $311.4$ bits for LL14 and HT14.
Hybrid attacks that guess part of the secret can exploit this structure, so the costs in Table~\ref{tab:sparse} are optimistic for SHIP's actual secret.
As a heuristic, we replaced the secret by a generic sparse ternary secret with the same entropy ($h=28$ for $N=2^{13}$, $h=27$ for $N=2^{14}$ and $2^{15}$) in the current estimator.
This lowers the estimates by $8$--$15$ bits (Table~\ref{tab:defaults}); for the paper's own bounds it gives $2^{110.8}$ $(13,55)$, $2^{110.6}$ $(14,100)$ and $2^{128.6}$ $(15,105)$ under the dual meet-in-the-middle hybrid.
Under this heuristic, HT14 and HT15 remain above $2^{133}$, but LL13 ($2^{119.2}$) and LL14 ($2^{122.5}$) do not reach $2^{128}$; for LL14 the remaining lever is a smaller $q_0$, which shrinks the gap $\gamma$ and costs precision (\S\ref{sec:eval}).
An entropy match is not a proof in either direction: an attacker may exploit the window structure more efficiently than the estimator's generic guessing, or less.
The paper's own criterion $(4w)^{0.4h}\ge 2^{115}$~\cite{May21,CHKS25} gives $2^{117.2}$ for LL13 ($2.2$ bits of margin), $2^{124.6}$ for LL14 and HT14 and $2^{137.0}$ for HT15.
Together, these results make the windowed secret, not the encapsulation modulus, the binding security constraint of the low-latency sets; a dedicated attack analysis remains future work.

\paragraph{Hardening the windowed secret}
The window width $w$ trades blind-rotation cost for entropy: the number of mux steps is $\lceil\log_4\lceil 2w/\theta\rceil\rceil$, and the secret's entropy is $h\log_2(4w)$.
Table~\ref{tab:window} measures this trade-off for LL14's primes and $\lgqp=65$ (digit-decomposed encapsulation).
At $w=396$ the entropy-matched model reaches $2^{128.3}$ under the dual attack for $28\%$ more latency and $20\%$ more key material; at $w=528$ it reaches $2^{133.0}$ for $47\%$ more latency.
Precision is unchanged.
For LL13, $w=264$ gives only $2^{124.8}$, because $\lgqp=42$ already limits the generic model to $2^{127.6}$.
We keep the paper's $w$ as the default and document $w=396$ for LL14 as the hardened choice.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\paragraph{Setup}
Apple M5 Pro laptop ($15$ cores, mixed performance and efficiency cores, on mains power), $48$\,GB RAM, macOS (arm64), AppleClang, OpenFHE~1.4.2 fork with \texttt{NATIVE\_SIZE=64}, \texttt{WITH\_REDUCED\_NOISE=ON} and OpenMP (Homebrew libomp, no thread pinning).
Inputs are uniform real vectors in $[-1,1]$ encrypted with the public key; precision is $\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ with $\varepsilon$ the maximum real error over all slots, as in~\cite{CHKS25}.
Latency excludes key generation.
OpenFHE caches its thread count at start-up, so every thread count runs in a separate process with \texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS} set.
Unless stated otherwise, measurements were taken without other heavy workloads; latencies are medians of $5$ runs (SHIP) or $3$ runs (OpenFHE), and precisions are given as ranges or medians over all runs.
Throughput follows~\cite[Eq.~(9)]{CHKS25}: slots $\times$ levels after bootstrapping $\times$ worst precision / median latency.
The latency measurements were taken before the digit-decomposed encapsulation became the default; the encapsulation step takes about $1$\,ms without and $3$--$5$\,ms with decomposition, so the tables are unaffected.

\paragraph{The LL13 gap}
With the paper's prime sizes (tree primes $24$ bits, $P$ $28$ bits), LL13 reached only $2.3$ bits, and the error was not caused by encryption noise: measured against the decryption of the input ciphertext, or with secret-key encryption (input error $8\times10^{-4}$ instead of $0.10$--$0.14$), it stayed at $2.4$ bits.
To locate the error, we varied one parameter at a time in an $N=2^{13}$ context outside the 128-bit budget (secret-key encryption).
Raising $p'$ changed little, whereas raising the auxiliary prime $P$ from $28$ to $40$--$60$ bits gave $3.2$--$3.6$ bits, and with the digit-decomposed encapsulation and a $60$-bit $P$, increasing the scale at a fixed gap $\gamma=2^5$ gave $4.5$--$5.2$, $6.3$--$6.7$ and $7.0$--$7.1$ bits for $\Delta=2^{20}$, $2^{22}$ and $2^{24}$, approaching the $7.3$-bit limit of the sine approximation.
LL13 is therefore limited by key-switching noise at the small auxiliary prime, not by the algorithm.
Within the same $218$-bit budget, moving one bit from each of the four product-tree primes to $P$ (tree primes $23$ bits, $P$ $34$ bits) raises LL13 from $2.5$--$2.7$ to $3.9$--$4.5$ bits with secret-key encryption (median $4.3$, paper $4.45$) and to $2.7$--$3.2$ bits with public-key encryption, where the input's encryption noise now dominates.
We adopted these sizes for the LL13 preset (Tables~\ref{tab:keys} and~\ref{tab:repro}); the same change does not help LL14 ($15.2$ bits with $P$ $2\times 52$ and $41$-bit tree primes).
A larger $q_0$ does not help either: with the scale fixed at $2^{20}$, every extra bit of $q_0$ cost about one bit of precision, because the output $\frac{\gamma}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi\mu/\gamma)$ amplifies the relative error of the product tree by the gap $\gamma=q_0/\Delta$.

\paragraph{High-throughput sets}
HT14 and HT15 do not fit in memory, so they ran with disk-backed factor keys (internal SSD) at four threads, two runs each, with the same bootstrapping code and the same $\lgqp$ as the defaults, while another application was running.
Their latencies are dominated by reading tens of gigabytes of keys and are not comparable with the paper.
HT15 reaches $18.3$--$18.4$ bits (paper: $18.1$) with nine levels left, in $49$--$51$\,s; key generation takes $354$\,s and the maximum RSS is $27.3$\,GiB, of which $8.6$\,GiB are resident keys.
HT14 reaches $4.0$ bits (paper: $4.55$) with nine levels, in $8$--$19$\,s, maximum RSS $14.4$\,GiB.
Precision and level counts match the paper; their speed needs a machine with enough memory.

\paragraph{Where the time goes}
For LL14, the factor loop takes $70\%$ of the latency on one thread ($3.72$\,s of $5.33$\,s), the S2C $23\%$ ($1.22$\,s) and the product tree $7\%$ ($0.39$\,s); encapsulation and conjugation are negligible.
From $1$ to $15$ threads, the factor loop speeds up $6.3\times$, the S2C $5.9\times$ and the tree $4.8\times$.
With $31$ factors on $15$ threads the factor loop runs in three waves on a mix of performance and efficiency cores, which limits the speed-up.
Over $1$, $2$, $4$, $8$, $12$ and $15$ threads, LL14 takes $5.28$, $3.18$, $1.45$, $1.11$, $0.89$ and $0.83$\,s, while the best equal-slot OpenFHE configuration takes $4.69$, $3.31$, $2.26$, $1.88$, $1.85$ and $1.67$\,s (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}).
Fitting Amdahl's law gives a serial fraction of $0.09$ for SHIP and $0.33$ for OpenFHE; on $32$ identical cores this would predict about $0.64$\,s and $1.63$\,s, but our cores are heterogeneous and we could not test a many-core server.

\subsection{Comparison with tuned OpenFHE bootstrapping}

\paragraph{Baseline choice}
A fair baseline must use the same security level, keep one level, and use current techniques.
With OpenFHE's default uniform ternary secret, \texttt{EvalBootstrap} needs $19$--$21$ levels; at $N=2^{15}$ decryption fails because of the approximation error, and at $N=2^{16}$ we obtained only $5.3$--$10.6$ bits.
We therefore use \texttt{SPARSE\_ENCAPSULATED}~\cite{BTH22}, the same encapsulation idea as SHIP; its sparse key is at $2^{149.7}$ ($N=2^{15}$) and $2^{172.1}$ ($N=2^{16}$) by the primal hybrid, i.e.\ more conservative than SHIP's.
OpenFHE does not support this mode together with composite scaling.
We tuned the level budget ($2$, $3$, $4$) at $N=2^{16}$ with OpenFHE's recommended $59/60$-bit scale, and measured both full packing ($32{,}768$ slots) and sparse packing with $8{,}192$ slots, the slot count of LL14.
For the best $8{,}192$-slot configuration we also varied $d_{\mathrm{num}}\in\{2,3,4,6\}$ and the scale ($50$, $55$, $59$ bits): at four threads the latency stayed between $2.20$ and $2.71$\,s, the default $d_{\mathrm{num}}$ and $59$-bit scale being within $3\%$ of the best, while a $50$-bit scale cost $8$ bits of precision.
At $N=2^{15}$, OpenFHE requires $\log_2(q_0/\Delta)\le 7$ and the $881$-bit budget then limits the scale to $45$ bits, which gives about $7$ bits of precision; HEaaN reaches $16.7$ bits at $N=2^{15}$ with a different parameter set~\cite[Table~3]{CHKS25}, so this limitation is specific to OpenFHE's configuration.
We did not evaluate meta-bootstrapping~\cite{BCCKK22}, which trades latency for precision, or SPRU~\cite{SPRU25}, which targets ciphertexts with few slots.

\paragraph{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:compare} and Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} show the results.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Latency.} Against the best OpenFHE configuration with the same $8{,}192$ slots (level budget $(3,3)$), LL14 is $0.89\times$ (slower) on one thread and $1.6\times$ and $2.0\times$ faster on $4$ and $15$ threads. Against the best full-packing configuration, it is $1.2\times$, $2.0\times$ and $2.1\times$ faster. SHIP's advantage therefore comes from parallelism: LL14 speeds up $6.4\times$ from $1$ to $15$ threads, OpenFHE $2.8$--$3.6\times$.
\item \textbf{Precision.} OpenFHE at $N=2^{16}$ has $2.4$--$3.8$ bits more ($18.6$--$20.1$ versus $16.0$--$16.5$).
\item \textbf{Throughput.} The best full-packing OpenFHE configuration is $3.9\times$ (one thread) and $2.2\times$ (15 threads) higher, because it refreshes four times as many slots. With equal slots, OpenFHE is $1.4\times$ higher on one thread and SHIP $1.6\times$ higher on $15$ threads.
\item \textbf{Memory and keys.} The tuned OpenFHE configurations need $9.4$--$9.6$\,GiB RSS against $13.8$\,GiB for LL14. Serialized keys are of similar size ($9.1$--$11.1$\,GiB for OpenFHE at level budget $(2,2)$, $10.2$\,GiB for LL14), and seed compression halves LL14's to $5.1$\,GiB; the same technique would apply to OpenFHE's keys.
\item \textbf{Ring degree.} SHIP runs at $N=2^{14}$ instead of $2^{16}$, so every operation outside bootstrapping on the same circuit is about four times cheaper in ring size; at $N=2^{15}$, OpenFHE reaches only $7$ bits in our configurations, whereas SHIP's HT15 reaches $18.3$ bits with nine levels.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Noise flooding}
When decryption results are shared, CKKS needs noise flooding for IND-CPA$^D$ security~\cite{LM21}.
OpenFHE's \texttt{NOISE\_FLOODING\_DECRYPT} mode requires its own scaling configuration with scales of about $2^{55}$ or more, which is incompatible with SHIP contexts.
We therefore emulated its computation: we estimate the noise from the imaginary parts of the bootstrapped output and add Gaussian noise of standard deviation $2^{s/2+\log_2\sqrt{12}+\text{estimate}}$ with statistical security $s=30$ and one query (OpenFHE's defaults).
OpenFHE estimates the noise in a separate run on a different ciphertext; our data-dependent estimate is only an approximation of that procedure and is not by itself an IND-CPA$^D$-secure decryption.
The flooding noise ($2^{21.4}$--$2^{22.2}$) is about $2^{17}$ times the output noise.
LL13 becomes meaningless (maximum error above $500$), LL14 drops from $16.0$ to $6.7$--$6.9$ bits and HT15 from $18.3$ to $7.4$ bits; the HT14 measurement was affected by a bug in the emulation and is omitted.
Applications that publish decryptions should raise the scale before flooding or budget for this loss.

%=====================================================================
\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
%=====================================================================

\paragraph{When to use SHIP}
On our machine, SHIP's measurable advantages over tuned OpenFHE bootstrapping are multi-threaded latency ($1.6$--$2.1\times$ at $4$--$15$ threads) and a four times smaller ring degree for the whole circuit.
It is not faster on one thread, not more precise, not more memory-efficient, and only more throughput-efficient than OpenFHE at equal slot counts with many threads.
The paper's larger speed-ups ($14$--$18\times$ from $1$ to $32$ cores) suggest that SHIP's advantage grows with the number of cores, which we could not test.

\paragraph{Threats to validity}
All measurements come from one laptop CPU with mixed core types, no thread pinning and a small number of runs ($3$--$5$ for latency, up to $15$ for precision); the first OpenFHE run is sometimes slower (no warm-up).
HT14 and HT15 were measured under concurrent load with disk-backed keys.
The baseline was tuned over level budgets, slot counts, $d_{\mathrm{num}}$ and scale sizes, but not with meta-bootstrapping, and OpenFHE's bootstrapping uses \texttt{FLEXIBLEAUTO} while SHIP uses \texttt{FIXEDMANUAL}.
Security estimates rest on two estimator versions with one cost model and ignore memory and the windowed secret structure.
The thread-scaling points at $2$, $8$ and $12$ threads were measured in a later session than those at $1$, $4$ and $15$, and the noise-flooding results come from an emulation.
Finally, the implementation supports \texttt{FIXEDMANUAL}, hybrid key switching and full packing only, and has not been independently reviewed.

%=====================================================================
\section{Related Work}
%=====================================================================

Conventional CKKS bootstrapping~\cite{CHKKS18} has been improved in its linear transforms and modular reduction~\cite{HK20,BMTH21}, its failure probability and key distribution~\cite{BTH22} and its precision~\cite{BCCKK22}; OpenFHE~\cite{OpenFHE22} implements these with sparse-secret encapsulation.
Recent low-latency designs reduce depth in different ways: bootstrapping bits~\cite{BCKS24} lowers precision requirements, SPRU~\cite{SPRU25} embeds $\Z_q$ into complex roots of unity and reports up to $5\times$ lower latency in OpenFHE for ciphertexts with few slots, and PaCo~\cite{PaCo25} reformulates decryption with blind rotations and a partial CoeffToSlot.
SHIP~\cite{CHKS25} targets full-slot ciphertexts.
On the security side, hybrid attacks have repeatedly lowered estimates for sparse-secret HE parameters~\cite{SC19,CP19}, and the HE security guidelines~\cite{SecGuide24} recommend checking such parameters with current tools; our findings are an instance of this pattern.

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%=====================================================================

We implemented SHIP as an OpenFHE feature with serialization, seed-compressed and disk-backed keys, ran the four parameter sets of the paper, and made changes that a general-purpose library needs: exact rounding in ModDown at small scales and exact output scales with role-specific primes. We also showed that the published sparse-key bounds for $N=2^{13}$ and $2^{14}$ fall short of $2^{128}$ under the dual meet-in-the-middle hybrid, and that a digit-decomposed encapsulation key tightens them by $13$--$55$ bits without precision loss, which leaves the windowed secret as the binding constraint.
Compared with OpenFHE's bootstrapping tuned with the same sparse-secret encapsulation, SHIP lowers multi-threaded latency by about $2\times$ at a four times smaller ring degree, but loses on single-thread latency, precision and throughput on our machine.
Future work includes a security analysis of the windowed secret,  less noisy key switching at small auxiliary primes, measurements on many-core servers, and noise flooding at small scales.

Environment variables: \texttt{SHIP\_FACTOR\_DIR} (disk-backed keys), \texttt{SHIP\_ENCAPS\_BITS} (size of $p'$), \texttt{SHIP\_SK\_ENCRYPT} (secret-key encryption), \texttt{SHIP\_SERIAL\_SIZE} (serialized key size) and \texttt{SHIP\_PROFILE} (per-phase times). \texttt{ship-baseline-bench} takes the scale bits, first-prime bits, trials, $N$, $d_{\mathrm{num}}$, level budget, secret distribution and slot count; \texttt{BASE\_SERIAL\_SIZE} prints the key size.
\end{itemize}
